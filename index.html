The Musical Written On Air

Alaha Husseinzadah





In 2020, there was a whirlwind of backlash by the Afghan community on social media concerning a musical that twisted the community’s notion of right and wrong. The Boy Who Danced on Air (Sohne & Rosser, 2013) imagines two young boys who have fallen prey to a practice in Afghanistan called bacha bazi. Bacha bazi, literally translated as “boy play”, occurs when young boys, who are abducted or sold, are sexually abused by wealthier men and used as dancing boys at gatherings. The Afghan community had issue with the lack of Afghan input in the production of this musical because the two white writers, librettist Charlie Sohne and composer Tim Rosser, have no connection to the people they spoke for. Through literary analysis, authoethnography, and memory I explore this activism the diaspora, including myself, took part in in 2020.


Although, I originally considered toning down the severity and criticism of the writers in order to compel the audience more effectively via logos, pathos, via an angry tone, is necessary to capture the seriousness of the issue and the reaction of actual Afghans to the musical’s grotesque representation.


Some scholars argue that the conversation surrounding the issue of bacha bazi has been more prominent in news media than in academic literature (Misra, 2023, p. 353), while others have argued that the issue has received greater awareness due to popular depictions of the practice in Khaled Hosseini’s The Kite Runner (Burmester, 2017, abstract). In some of the academic literature, authors have approached the issue of bacha bazi nonmorally similar to Sohne and Rosser. For example, Baily glazes over the issue only as a dancing practice and doesn’t comment on the gravity of the situation or the ethics involved in the practice thereby ignoring victims' perspectives (2013). This ignorance in the West could be correlated to the literal translation of bacha bazi as “boy play” in much of the academic and popular literature, which flattens the tension associated with the word.


In the section “Bacha Bazi: Amusement or Deception?”, I look at the loaded meaning of bacha bazi for Afghans. Although the literal translation “boy play” represents one meaning of the term in Farsi, educating Western audiences with a more multidimensional understanding of the term is necessary to capture the cultural milieu the term is situated in—a milieu overlooked by Sohne and Rosser. Looking at issues in translation and how we can find meaning in words across languages using lived experience may uncover this milieu.


In entering the conversation about bacha bazi, I am not only in conversation with Sohne and Rosser but also the whole Afghan community and with the broader Western audience of the musical. Bacha Bazi is a particular issue because of its prevalence in the context of Afghanistan. However, pedophilia has affected all corners of the globe transhistorically, with one example documented in some versions of the western mythology of Ganymede. At the same time, within the field of gender and sexuality studies and postcolonial studies the issues covered in this essay not only pertain to bacha bazi but an incongruence of gender and sexuality across geography revealed in Sohne and Rosser’s appropriation, despite their attempt at the opposite.


Looking into future research into bacha bazi and its victims, it would be important to incorporate research from scholars with survivor positionalities. Their reception and thoughts on the musical may offer an insider’s voice within the literature that resists harmful representations of their lived experience. This perspective would strengthen analysis of the musical and would fill my identity gap within this essay as I am not male or a survivor of bacha bazi.


The Boy Who Danced On Air was written on air: it is not grounded in Afghan culture, lived experiences, or language. Through ignorance Sohne and Rosser wrote a shallow narrative, which holds up a mirror to the West rather than Afghanistan itself and is therefore deeply orientalist.
